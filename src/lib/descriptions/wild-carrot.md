Wild carrot, or Queen Anne's lace, is the wild ancestor of the carrots in your garden. In summer it fills fields and roadsides with flat, lacy white flower heads. Look in the centre of the head and there's often a single dark purple flower, said to be a drop of blood where Queen Anne pricked her finger while making lace.

It's a lovely plant, but it belongs to a family with some of the deadliest plants in North America. Learn it carefully.

## How to spot it

Check every one of these before you pick:

- **The stem is hairy.** This is the most important feature. An old saying helps you remember: "Queen Anne has hairy legs."
- **Leaves** are finely cut and feathery, just like carrot tops, and hairy underneath.
- **Flowers** form a flat, lacy umbrella of tiny white flowers, often with one purple flower in the middle. Long, forked green bracts stick out underneath.
- **Seed heads** curl up into a cup as they ripen, like a bird's nest.
- **The smell** of the crushed root and leaves is unmistakably carrot.

## What it's good for

Dig the root of a first-year plant in autumn or early spring. It's thin, pale and woody, but it smells and tastes like carrot. You can batter and fry the flower heads, and many beneficial insects visit them.

## Deadly lookalikes

- **Poison hemlock** has a smooth, hairless stem with purple blotches and a musty, mousy smell. It can grow over 2 m tall. Every part of it is deadly.
- **Water hemlock** has a smooth stem and grows in wet ground. It's one of the most toxic plants in North America.

If you aren't completely sure, leave it alone.
