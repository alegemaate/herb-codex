Horsetail is a living fossil. Its relatives have been around for over 350 million years, and some of them once grew as tall as trees. Today's horsetail is much smaller, but it's just as hard to kill. Its roots run deep and spread far, so any piece left behind grows back.

It doesn't flower at all. It reproduces from spores, like ferns.

## How to spot it

- **In early spring**, pale brown stems with a small cone on top pop up first. They release their spores and then wither.
- **Green stems** follow, hollow and jointed, with rings of thin, needle-like branches. They look like a bottlebrush or a horse's tail.
- **Joints** pull apart with a little pop and fit back together like a toy.
- **The stems feel rough**, because they're full of silica.

## What it's good for

That silica makes the stems gritty. People used bundles of horsetail to scrub pots and polish wood, which earned it the name "scouring rush".

Herbalists use a tea of the green stems as a diuretic and as a source of silica for hair, skin, nails and bones. It's meant for short-term use only.

## Don't confuse it with

Marsh horsetail looks almost identical, grows in wetter ground and is toxic to livestock. Telling the two apart takes a close look and some experience.
