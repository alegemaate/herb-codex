Flowering dogwood is one of the loveliest small trees in eastern North America. In early spring, before its leaves open, it's covered in what look like big white flowers. In autumn, the leaves turn deep red and the branches fill with shiny red berries.

It's the state tree of both Virginia and Missouri.

## How to spot it

- **The "flowers"** aren't really flowers. The four white "petals" are bracts. The true flowers are the small greenish-yellow cluster in the middle.
- **Leaves** are oval and grow in pairs. Their veins curve up towards the tip. Gently pull a leaf in half and fine, stretchy threads will hold the two pieces together.
- **Bark** on older trees breaks into small square blocks, a bit like alligator skin.
- **Berries** are bright red and grow in tight clusters in autumn.
- **Size** is usually 6–12 m, with a wide, flat-topped crown.

## What it's good for

Birds love the berries, which are rich in fat, and eat them to fuel up for migration. People don't eat them, because they're bitter.

Indigenous peoples and early settlers made a bark tea to treat fevers, and it was used as a quinine substitute during the American Civil War. The wood is very hard and was used for tool handles and the shuttles on weaving looms.

## Other dogwoods

Shrub dogwoods, like red-osier and grey dogwood, are common at field edges. Some of the photos above show one. They have the same curved leaf veins, but they have small flat flower clusters without the big white bracts, and white or blue berries.
