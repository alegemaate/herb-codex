Yarrow is one of the oldest medicinal herbs we know of. Its Latin name, _Achillea_, comes from the legend that the Greek hero Achilles used it to treat his soldiers' wounds. It still grows almost everywhere in the Northern Hemisphere: in fields, on roadsides and in gardens.

Wild yarrow has white flowers. The pink one in the photo is a garden form of the same species.

## How to spot it

- **Leaves** are soft and feathery, cut into many tiny segments. The name _millefolium_ means "thousand leaves".
- **Flowers** are small daisies, each about 5 mm across, packed into flat-topped clusters.
- **The smell** is strong and spicy when you crush a leaf.
- **Stems** are tough, upright and a little hairy, 30–90 cm tall.

## What it's good for

Yarrow's best-known use is to stop bleeding. Crushed fresh leaves have been pressed onto small cuts for thousands of years, which gave it names like "soldier's woundwort" and "nosebleed".

As a tea, it's a traditional remedy for colds and fevers, often mixed with elderflower and peppermint. Young leaves are edible in small amounts and have a bitter, peppery taste.

Many small bees, wasps and hoverflies visit the flowers.

## Don't confuse it with

Poison hemlock, water hemlock and other members of the carrot family also have flat clusters of tiny white flowers. Look closely: yarrow's flowers are miniature daisies, and its leaves are fine, soft and feathery.
